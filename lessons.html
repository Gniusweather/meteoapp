The Observer's Role
WMO-No.8 §1.1 · WMO-No.49 Vol I Part I Reg 1
Surface weather observations provide the primary data for numerical weather prediction (NWP), aviation safety, climate records, and the Global Telecommunication System (GTS).
Main observation elements
  • Air temperature and dew point / relative humidity
  • Wind direction and speed (10-min mean)
  • Visibility (MOR — Meteorological Optical Range)
  • Present and past weather (ww, W1, W2)
  • Cloud amount (N), type (CL, CM, CH) and base height (h)
  • Atmospheric pressure at station level and MSL
  • Precipitation amount and type
Synoptic observation hours (UTC)
TypeTimes (UTC)
Main synoptic00 · 06 · 12 · 18

Aviation (METAR)Hourly (or 30-min at busy aerodromes)

Timeliness is mandatory. WMO-No.49 requires observations to be transmitted within 15 minutes of the main synoptic hour. A late or missing observation cannot be reconstructed — it is simply lost from the global record.
02
Temperature
WMO-No.8 Ch.2 · WMO-No.306 FM-12 group 1SnTTT
Stevenson Screen requirements (WMO-No.8 §2.2)
  • Height of thermometer bulbs: 1.25 m above ground level
  • Screen painted white, double-louvred, door facing away from equator (north in Southern Hemisphere, south in Northern — TNCA/TNCB/TNCC face north as they are in NH)
  • Sited on short natural grass; no artificial surfaces directly below
  • Minimum distance from obstructions: ≥ 4× the height of that obstruction
Accuracy and resolution
  • Uncertainty goal: ±0.3°C (WMO-No.8 Table 2.1)
  • Resolution: 0.1°C
  • Maximum temperature: read at 18:00 LST (or observation hour), reset immediately after
  • Minimum temperature: read at 06:00 LST (or observation hour), reset immediately after

03
Humidity & Dew Point
WMO-No.8 Ch.4 · FM-12 group 2SnTdTdTd
Dew point temperature (Td) is the temperature to which air must be cooled at constant pressure for saturation to occur (RH = 100%). It is always ≤ air temperature.
Psychrometer method (WMO-No.8 §4.2)
  • Dry-bulb (T): measures air temperature directly
  • Wet-bulb (Tw): covered with muslin wick kept moist with distilled water; cooling from evaporation lowers reading
  • Depression = T − Tw; enter psychrometric tables with T and depression to obtain Td and RH
  • Wick must be changed every 2–4 weeks; always use distilled or de-ionised water
Key relationships
  • T = Td → RH = 100% (fog / saturation possible)
  • T − Td < 3°C → high humidity, fog risk
  • T − Td > 15°C → very dry air
  • Typical Curaçao: Td ≈ 22–26°C, RH ≈ 75–85%
SYNOP encoding — group 2SnTdTdTd
Same encoding as temperature: prefix 2, then Sn (0=positive, 1=negative), then |Td|×10 in 3 digits.
Example: Td = 26.7°C → 20267  |  Td = −1.2°C → 21012
Alternative: if only RH available, use group 29UUU (U = RH in %).
04
Wind
WMO-No.8 Ch.5 · WMO-No.49 Reg 12.3 · FM-12 Nddff
Wind direction is reported as the direction from which the wind blows, in degrees true (not magnetic), measured clockwise from 000° (north). A southerly wind blows from the south → 180°.
Averaging periods (WMO-No.8 §5.8)
  • SYNOP: 10-minute mean speed and direction at observation time
  • METAR: 2-minute mean speed; gusts = maximum 3-second mean during the preceding 10 minutes
  • Gust reported when max 3-sec wind ≥ mean + 5 kt (or ≥ 15 kt above mean)
Measurement standard
  • Height: 10 m above the surrounding terrain (WMO-No.8 §5.9)
  • Exposure: no obstacles within 150 m upwind; obstacle height < 1/10 of distance
  • 1 m/s = 1.944 kt  |  1 kt = 0.514 m/s
Special cases
  • Calm: speed < 0.3 m/s (< 1 kt) → dd=00, ff=00 in SYNOP; CALM in METAR
  • Variable: no predominant direction → dd=99 in SYNOP; VRB in METAR
  • Speed ≥ 99 units: ff=99 in Nddff group, followed by 00fff extension with 3-digit speed
SYNOP encoding (dd and ff)
dd = direction ÷ 10 (round to nearest 10°, then ÷ 10). Example: 100° → dd = 10. ff = speed in m/s or kn (iw determines units).

05
Visibility
WMO-No.8 Ch.9 · FM-12 VV · FM-15 (METAR)
Meteorological Optical Range (MOR) — the length of path in the atmosphere required to reduce the luminous flux of a beam from an incandescent lamp at 2700 K to 5% of its original value (Koschmieder's Law). MOR defines visibility independent of day/night.
Prevailing visibility
The greatest visibility observed over at least half the horizon circle. For SYNOP, report this in the VV code. For METAR, report the prevailing visibility in metres (≥10 km → 9999).
Observation method
  • Establish a set of reference objects (landmarks, lights) at documented distances around the station
  • During daytime: dark objects against sky background
  • Night-time: lights of known intensity; reduce daytime visibility estimate by ~10%
  • Ceilometer/transmissometer gives automated MOR readings

METAR visibility
  • Reported in whole metres, rounded down to the nearest reportable value
  • ≥ 10 km: reported as 9999
  • RVR (Runway Visual Range) reported separately when < 1500 m
  • SPECI trigger: vis drops below or recovers above 1500 m, 3000 m or a stated aerodrome minimum

METAR cloud reporting rules
  • Report each distinct cloud layer in ascending order of base height
  • Cb and TCU must be explicitly stated: BKN025CB, FEW015TCU
  • If SKC: report SKC (no cloud, no CB); NCD = no cloud detected (automated)

Cb triggers SPECI — cumulonimbus on approach/departure path regardless of sky coverage. Always identify and report Cb (thunderstorm cloud) explicitly.

Key distinctions
  • Drizzle: drop diameter < 0.5 mm; uniform; falls from stratus
  • Rain: drop diameter ≥ 0.5 mm; falls from any cloud with sufficient depth

Atmospheric Pressure
WMO-No.8 Ch.3 · FM-12 groups 3PPPP, 4PPPP, 5appp
Pressure definitions
TermDefinition
QFEActual pressure at the aerodrome/instrument level. Aircraft altimeter reads 0 ft at aerodrome elevation when set to QFE.
QNHICAO altimeter setting: pressure reduced to sea level using ISA (standard atmosphere 15°C, −6.5°C/km lapse). Aircraft altimeter reads aerodrome elevation when set to QNH. Used in METAR.
QFF (MSL)Station pressure reduced to sea level using the actual mean temperature of the air column. More accurate than QNH for synoptic analysis. Used in SYNOP group 4PPPP.
Standard atmosphere: 1013.25 hPa, 15°C at MSL. The mean sea-level pressure for synoptic analysis (isobars on weather maps) uses QFF, not QNH.
Accuracy requirements (WMO-No.8 §3.11)
  • Uncertainty: ±0.1 hPa
  • Resolution: 0.1 hPa
  • Barometers calibrated against primary standard; verified at each observation

Diurnal pressure oscillation (tropical)
At low-latitude stations (Curaçao, Aruba, Bonaire) pressure follows a consistent diurnal cycle: two maxima at ~10:00 and ~22:00 LST, two minima at ~04:00 and ~16:00 LST, amplitude ≈ ±1 hPa. Deviations from this pattern can indicate approaching disturbances.

Precipitation Measurement
WMO-No.8 Ch.6 · FM-12 group 6RRRt
Gauge types (WMO-No.8 §6.2)
  • Standard storage gauge: cylindrical receiver, 200 mm diameter; read with measuring cylinder graduated to 0.1 mm; at each synoptic hour
  • Tipping-bucket gauge: automated; each tip = 0.1 mm (or 0.2 mm); calibration essential — heavier rainfall rates may cause under-catch
  • Weighing gauge: continuous; can measure snow and solid precipitation

Station Exposure & Inspection
WMO-No.8 §1.3–1.5 · WMO-No.49 Appendix 1.B
The quality of any observation is directly limited by the quality of instrument exposure. A perfect instrument in a bad location gives a bad observation.
Temperature / humidity screen
  • Height: thermometer bulbs at 1.25 m AGL
  • Surface: short natural grass (not concrete, sand, or gravel)
  • Clearance: no obstruction within a horizontal distance of 4× its height
  • Screen: white paint maintained; hinges and latches in working order; aspirated where natural ventilation is insufficient
Wind anemometer
  • Height: 10 m AGL on a mast
  • Fetch: no obstacle upwind closer than 10× its height for the prevailing wind direction; no obstacle taller than 1/10 of its distance
  • At aerodromes: representative of the runway threshold / middle
Precipitation gauge
  • Rim height: 30 cm above ground
  • Distance from nearest obstacle: ≥ 2× its height
  • Level: gauge must be truly vertical (use spirit level)
Visibility
  • Observer position: unobstructed horizontal view over ≥ 180° of the horizon
  • Reference points: documented list of objects/lights with known azimuths and distances
  • Automated transmissometer: path length typically 75 m; needs calibration verification
Daily inspection checklist
  • Check all instrument readings are within expected range
  • Inspect Stevenson screen: clean louvers, fresh wick on wet-bulb
  • Empty and measure rain gauge; check tipping-bucket mechanism
  • Verify anemometer rotation (cup type); check data logger time
  • Confirm barometer zero correction is applied; compare with backup barometer
  • Record any anomalies, maintenance, or site changes in the station log

Vaisala AviMet — Automated Aviation Weather System
ICAO Annex 3 · Annex 10 Vol II · Doc 9328 · Doc 8896 · Doc 9837 · WMO-No.306
Vaisala AviMet is an automated aviation weather observing and message-generation system used at airports and by air navigation service providers (ANSPs). It integrates field sensors — wind, RVR/visibility, ceilometer (cloud), present-weather detector, temperature/dew point, and barometric pressure — with a processing unit that applies ICAO/WMO algorithms to compile and disseminate MET reports (METAR, SPECI, and local routine/special reports) to ATC, pilots, and the aeronautical fixed telecommunication network in real time.
Why standards compliance matters
An automated system is only as trustworthy as the standards it follows: sensor siting and accuracy, averaging periods, rounding rules, and message formatting must all match what pilots and ATC are trained to expect. AviMet's software is built and certified against a specific set of ICAO and WMO reference documents, so its output is interchangeable with human-observed reports anywhere in the world.
International recommendations applied to AviMet software
DocumentWhat it covers
Annex 3 (2020, 20th ed., Amdt 80)ICAO's core standard for meteorological service to air navigation — defines what MET reports, TAF, SIGMET/AIRMET and warnings must contain and the technical limits (rounding, units, averaging periods) they must meet. The backbone standard AviMet is built to satisfy.
Annex 10 Vol II (2016, 7th ed.)Aeronautical Telecommunications — communication procedures. Governs how MET messages are formatted and transmitted over ATS/AFTN message-handling networks, so AviMet's output reaches ATC and other systems correctly.
Doc 9328 (2005, 3rd ed.)Manual of Runway Visual Range Observing and Reporting Practices — siting, calibration, and reporting practice for the transmissometers/forward-scatter sensors AviMet uses to derive RVR.
Doc 8896 (2019, 12th ed.)Manual of Aeronautical Meteorological Practice — practical guidance that expands on Annex 3, covering how aerodrome observation, forecasting, and warning services should actually be implemented day to day.
WMO-No.306 (2011 ed., updated 2019)Manual on Codes, Vol I.1 — defines the FM-15 METAR/SPECI and FM-51 TAF code forms (and FM-12 SYNOP) that AviMet must encode its observations into, plus the underlying code tables (ww, N, CL/CM/CH, VV).
Doc 9837 (2011, 2nd ed.)Manual on Automatic Meteorological Observing Systems at Aerodromes — the document most specific to AviMet's job: sensor siting/accuracy requirements and the algorithms for deriving present weather, cloud, and visibility automatically, without a human observer.
Document summaries
Annex 3 — Meteorological Service for International Air Navigation (20th ed., 2020, Amdt 80)
ICAO's principal standard governing all meteorological service provided to international air navigation. It specifies the MET reports required at aerodromes (METAR/SPECI), the content and format of TAF, SIGMET/AIRMET, aerodrome warnings, and volcanic-ash/tropical-cyclone advisories. It sets the technical envelope every observation — automated or human — must satisfy: units, rounding rules, observing/reporting cycles, and the criteria that trigger a SPECI. Appendix 3 of the Annex defines the exact METAR/SPECI/TAF templates that a system like AviMet must reproduce.
Annex 10 Vol II — Aeronautical Telecommunications, Communication Procedures (7th ed., 2016)
Covers the technical and procedural standards for aeronautical telecommunications; Volume II specifically addresses communication procedures — the format, addressing, and priority handling of messages sent over the Aeronautical Fixed Telecommunication Network (AFTN) and ATS message-handling systems. For AviMet, this governs how a generated METAR/SPECI is packaged and transmitted so ATC, other aerodromes, and international exchange systems receive it correctly and promptly.
Doc 9328 — Manual of Runway Visual Range Observing and Reporting Practices (3rd ed., 2005)
Detailed technical guidance on RVR: siting requirements for transmissometers and forward-scatter meters along the runway, calibration and maintenance practices, the algorithms used to convert raw extinction-coefficient readings into a reportable RVR value, and how multiple observation points (touchdown, midpoint, stop-end) should be combined and reported. AviMet's RVR-derivation logic is built to match this manual's practices.
Doc 8896 — Manual of Aeronautical Meteorological Practice (12th ed., 2019)
The practical companion to Annex 3 — where the Annex sets the "what," this manual explains the "how." It gives operational guidance for aerodrome meteorological offices and observing systems: how to organize observation and forecasting services, coordinate with ATC, structure warnings, and apply the Annex 3 requirements in day-to-day practice. It is the reference used when implementing or auditing an automated system like AviMet against real operational needs.
WMO-No.306 — Manual on Codes, Vol I.1 (Annex II to WMO Technical Regulations, 2011 ed., updated 2019)
WMO's master reference for meteorological code forms. Volume I.1 defines FM-15 METAR/SPECI and FM-51 TAF (alongside FM-12 SYNOP), specifying every group's exact digit/letter structure and the full set of code tables used within them — present weather ww, cloud amount N, cloud genus CL/CM/CH, visibility VV, and more. AviMet's message-encoding engine is built directly against these code-form definitions so its output is machine- and human-readable anywhere in the world.
Doc 9837 — Manual on Automatic Meteorological Observing Systems at Aerodromes (2nd ed., 2011)
The document most specific to AviMet's actual job. It covers the requirements for automated (as opposed to human-observed) meteorological systems at aerodromes: sensor siting and accuracy specifications, the averaging and sampling periods each sensor type must use, and — critically — the algorithms an automated system must apply to derive present weather, cloud amount/height, and visibility without a human observer physically watching the sky. This is the technical backbone that makes unmanned or lightly-manned automated stations possible.
Observer takeaway. Even at a fully automated station, the human observer's role doesn't disappear — cross-checking AviMet output against what you actually see (per Lesson 1–10) is exactly how sensor faults, siting problems, or software bugs get caught before a bad report reaches a pilot.

Cloud Types — High-altitude genera (Cirrus, Cirrocumulus, Cirrostratus, Cumulonimbus), mid-altitude (Nimbostratus, Altocumulus, Altostratus), and low-altitude (Cumulus, Stratocumulus, Stratus). Cumulonimbus spans all levels. Reference: WMO-No.407 International Cloud Atlas.
Beaufort Wind Scale (0–12) — Visual sea-state reference with corresponding wind speeds in mph, knots, and km/h. Force 0 = Calm (<1 kt). Force 7 = Near gale (28–33 kt). Force 12 = Hurricane (≥64 kt). WMO-No.8 §5.4.
